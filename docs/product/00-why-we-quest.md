# Why we Quest

You are still the English teacher. The Quest does not replace your judgement, your lesson, your assessment, or your voice. It gives the hour a **world the children can see** — so effort has a name, contribution leaves a trace, and the year has peaks worth remembering.

## The problem we wanted to solve

A classroom contains more kinds of success than a test score can show. Academic learning matters and must remain visible. But if recognition follows only the highest mark, many children have very few visible ways to contribute: helping a partner, trying again, listening carefully, including someone, finding a creative way into the language, or staying with a difficult task.

The Great Class Quest was created to widen that lens. It gives the teacher a consistent way to notice and reward **observable behaviour, life skills, effort, growth, and belonging** alongside academic learning. It is not an anti-academic system and it is not a promise that every child will respond in the same way. It is a classroom layer designed to make more meaningful moments visible and worth talking about.

## A gameful world around the lesson

The Quest is an engaging, year-round ecosystem rather than a collection of disconnected buttons. Clouds, maps, houses, rituals, stories, progression, choice, and a small economy give everyday English a shared continuity. A lesson can feed a class journey, a student’s personal path, a guild identity, a story, and the next adventure.

The goal is not for children to sit and play the application by themselves. Students do not log in. They see the Quest on the classroom screen — clouds, map, houses, celebrations, and stories — while you run it from the classroom PC. The gameful layer serves the lesson; the lesson never becomes an excuse to press buttons.

## You are the Quest Master

Stars are not stickers on a chart, and they are not an official grade. They are **specific recognition you choose to give** when a child does something worth naming. The teacher remains the professional who observes the room, decides what counts, explains why, and adapts the practice to the class.

On every plan, **you** decide who earns stars. On Elite, AI may write the day’s diary, paint a picture, or suggest a Chronicle page — and you still keep, edit, or ignore it. The adventure serves the English classroom. The classroom does not serve the adventure.

## More than one way to be seen

The four Award Stars reasons are **soft skills / life skills** that the English lesson can name *as they happen*. They are not grammar marks and not labels such as “good child”.

| Life skill | What you are naming |
|------------|---------------------|
| **Teamwork** | Helping, pairing, listening, and making space for a classmate |
| **Creativity** | A new idea, a surprising sentence, or playful use of the language |
| **Respect** | Kindness, inclusion, care for people, and care for the room |
| **Focus** | Effort, attention, self-regulation, and staying with a hard task |

The strongest classroom language points to the action, not to a personality label. “You waited, listened, and brought your partner into the answer — Teamwork” is more useful than “You are wonderful.” This is the logic of behaviour-specific and process-focused feedback: make the next helpful action easier to recognise and repeat ([Hattie & Timperley, 2007](https://doi.org/10.3102/003465430298487); [IES WWC, 2024](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/PracticeGuide/31/Published)).

The wider Quest creates other legitimate routes to recognition. **Welcome Back** honours a return after absence. **Teacher Boon** can name character that the four everyday reasons do not quite capture. **Hero’s Boon (peer gift)** makes generosity visible under clear fairness rules. None of these turns a child into a permanent label; they mark a moment or a pattern that the class can understand.

## Academic learning still belongs here

GCQ broadens recognition; it does not remove academic purpose. The English classroom still teaches language, literacy, knowledge, and confidence. **Scholar’s Scroll** supports tests and dictations, **Quiz of the Week** supports retrieval and review, **Story Weavers** makes writing communal, and **Quest Assignment** carries learning into the next lesson.

The distinction matters: a child does not need to be the highest-scoring student to be a valuable member of the class, and a life-skill star is not a substitute for academic feedback. The two strands can strengthen one another when the teacher keeps each one honest.

## Why these design choices

The design is informed by several complementary ideas in teaching and motivation. These sources support the principles behind the design; they are not evidence that GCQ itself has already been experimentally validated.

| Design principle | What it means in the Quest |
|------------------|---------------------------|
| **Autonomy** | Children make bounded choices: a Hero Path, a branch in the Skill Tree, a Gold purchase, or a contribution to a shared story. |
| **Competence** | Progress becomes visible through clouds, maps, levels, auras, stories, and fair milestones — not only through a hidden gradebook. |
| **Relatedness** | A child belongs to a class, a guild, a story, and a school community that can include children they do not meet in every lesson. |
| **Structured collaboration** | Shared class goals, Guild Hall, Bounties, and Story Weavers make participation social; the teacher still structures the task so quieter or less confident children can take part. |
| **Purposeful feedback** | Award Stars name an observable action in the moment, while Hero’s Chronicle helps the teacher notice patterns over time. |

Self-Determination Theory identifies autonomy, competence, and relatedness as basic needs that support active and positive involvement in learning ([Niemiec & Ryan, 2009](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1477878509104318); [SDT in education](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/topics/application-education/)). Guidance on collaborative learning likewise stresses structure and shared outcomes: competition can help a group work together, but too much emphasis on winning can pull attention away from learning ([EEF](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/teaching-learning-toolkit/collaborative-learning-approaches?search_term=collab)).

Gamification can support cognitive, motivational, and behavioural outcomes, but effects vary by design and context. That is why GCQ combines competition with cooperation, progression with reflection, and spectacle with teacher judgement; it does not treat points or prizes as a universal solution ([Sailer & Homner, 2020](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-019-09498-w)).

## How the ecosystem works together

| Layer | Features | What the layer is for |
|-------|----------|-----------------------|
| **Notice** | Award Stars, Bounties, Welcome Back | Name an observable contribution, effort, return, or shared class goal. |
| **Belong** | Team Quest, Guild Hall, Hero’s Boon | Give the class and the wider school a shared identity without making one ranking the whole story. |
| **Grow** | Hero Path, Skill Tree, Gold, Mystic Market, Familiars | Turn progress into choice, identity, patience, and a visible long-term relationship with the Quest. |
| **Remember** | Adventure Log, Hero’s Chronicle, ceremonies | Give the lesson a memory, give the teacher a private record, and give the year meaningful peaks. |
| **Create and learn** | Scholar’s Scroll, Quiz of the Week, Story Weavers, Quest Assignment | Keep language learning, practice, writing, and continuity at the centre. |

One Award Star can feed several parts of the world, but each feature has a different job. Team Quest is a monthly class race. Hero’s Challenge is a monthly student race. Guild Hall is the year-long house identity. **Ceremony of the Month**, **Hero of the Day**, and **Grand Guild Ceremony** are different rituals with different meanings.

## The teacher’s Chronicle starts on day one

**Hero’s Chronicle** is the teacher’s private notebook from the first day of September. It sits alongside the roster and the daily classroom routine. Use it to record observations, patterns, goals, strategies, and meaningful moments; publish only what you deliberately choose to share. It is not a new student-facing feature and it is not the public Adventure Log.

The daily classroom backbone is simple: teach, notice, award when appropriate, keep the next task visible, and close the lesson with the Adventure Log when the plan includes it. The rest of the ecosystem adds depth gradually; it should never make a good lesson harder to run.

## A year with peaks, not a flat scoreboard

The year has a rhythm because continuity helps a class build shared meaning:

- **Each lesson** can leave an Adventure Log, a specific recognition, and a next quest.
- **Each month** can bring a Ceremony of the Month, with a class story and a student story.
- **The school year** can build a Guild Hall identity, evolving paths, stories, and relationships.
- **June** can close the journey with the Grand Guild Ceremony and a record of how the community grew.

This is why the suggested staff-training timeline introduces one new layer at a time. A staged introduction reduces overload, gives teachers time to practise and revisit a routine, and leaves room for professional judgement. It is a suggested route, not an app requirement: once a teacher feels comfortable, they can adopt, combine, or experiment with features earlier or later. This approach reflects evidence-informed professional development guidance on manageable sequencing, planning, practice, and embedding new routines ([EEF Effective Professional Development](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/guidance-reports/effective-professional-development%20)).

## Guardrails that protect the purpose

- Stars recognise observable actions and growth; they are not an official grade or a judgement of a child’s worth.
- Academic progress remains visible through its own tools; life-skill recognition does not replace subject feedback.
- Gold is separate from rank. Children can spend it, but they cannot buy a higher place on a leaderboard.
- Class Bounties and Guild Power reward shared participation, not only the child who is already ahead.
- Hero’s Boon has eligibility and repeat-use rules so generosity does not become popularity pressure.
- Fair rotation, co-recognition, and Teacher Boon help prevent one narrow definition of success from owning every celebration.
- Hero’s Chronicle remains private unless the teacher chooses to publish a page. AI suggestions remain editable and optional.
- If the gameful layer distracts from learning, simplify it. The teacher can always return to the honest core of the lesson.

## What success looks like in practice

Success is not “everyone wins every leaderboard”. It looks more like this:

- Children can describe a helpful action in English, not just a score.
- More than one kind of contribution becomes visible across the month.
- Students learn to notice classmates’ effort and include one another.
- The teacher has a clearer, more humane record of patterns and next steps.
- Academic practice and classroom culture reinforce one another without becoming the same thing.
- The class carries a shared story from September to June and still has room for new features, new ideas, and teacher-led experiments.

The educational sources above ground design choices such as autonomy-supportive teaching, observable feedback, structured collaboration, social-emotional development, and gradual professional learning. They do not constitute a product evaluation of The Great Class Quest. Use professional judgement, observe your own classes, and adapt the system to the children in front of you.
